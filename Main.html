History

International Mother Language Day is observed annually on 21 February to promote linguistic diversity, multilingualism, and cultural heritage, as declared by UNESCO in 1999. This date commemorates the 1952 sacrifices in Dhaka for the recognition of the Bangla language, highlighting the importance of mother tongues in education and sustainable societies.

21 February was declared to be International Mother Language Day by UNESCO on 17 November 1999. It has been observed throughout the world since 21 February 2000. The declaration came up in tribute to the Language Movement done by the Bangladeshis (then the East Pakistanis).

UNESCO chooses a theme for each International Mother Language Day and sponsors related events at its Paris headquarters. In 2008, the International Year of Languages began on International Mother Language Day. It is celebrated in Chile, Russia, the Philippines, Egypt, and Canada.

In Bangladesh, 21 February 1952 is the anniversary of the day when the Bengalis of the then-Pakistani province of East Bengal (which is now the independent state of Bangladesh) fought for recognition of their Bengali language as an official central state language instead of a provincial language.[7] The day is also observed by Indian Bengalis in the states of West Bengal, Assam, Jharkhand, and Tripura, as well as in the union territory of the Andaman and Nicobar Islands.

When Pakistan was created in 1947, it had two geographically separate parts: East Pakistan (currently known as Bangladesh) and West Pakistan (currently known as Pakistan). The two parts were very different from each other in the sense of culture and language, and were also separated by India in between.

In 1948, the government of Pakistan declared Urdu to be the sole national language of Pakistan, even though Bengali, or Bangla, was spoken by the majority of people combining East Pakistan and West Pakistan. The East Pakistan people protested since the majority of the population was from East Pakistan and their mother language was Bangla. They demanded Bangla to be at least one of the national languages, in addition to Urdu. The demand was raised first by Dhirendranath Datta from East Pakistan on 23 February 1948, in the Constituent Assembly of Pakistan.

To demolish the protest, the government of Pakistan outlawed public meetings and rallies. The students of the University of Dhaka, with the support of the public, arranged massive rallies and meetings. On 21 February 1952, police opened fire on rallies. Abdus Salam, Abul Barkat, Rafiq Uddin Ahmed, Abdul Jabbar, and Shafiur Rahman died, with hundreds of others injured. This was a rare incident in history where people sacrificed their lives for their mother tongue.

Since then, Bangladeshis celebrate the International Mother Language Day as one of their tragic days. They visit the Shaheed Minar, a monument built in memory of the martyrs, and its replicas to express their deep sorrow, respect. and gratitude to them.

Recognition

International Mother Language Day is a national holiday in Bangladesh. The resolution was suggested by Rafiqul Islam and Abdus Salam, Bengalis living in Vancouver, Canada. They wrote a letter to Kofi Annan on 9 January 1998 asking him to take a step to save the world's languages from extinction by declaring an International Mother Language Day. Rafiq proposed the date as 21 February to commemorate the 1952 killings in Dhaka during the Language Movement.

Rafiqul Islam's proposal was introduced in the Bangladesh Parliament, and in due course (at the behest of the Prime Minister), a formal proposal was submitted to UNESCO by the Government of Bangladesh. The process of shepherding the proposal through UNESCO's regulatory system was undertaken by Syed Muazzem Ali, then Bangladesh ambassador to France and Permanent Representative to UNESCO, and Tozammel Tony Huq, his predecessor, who was then a Special Adviser to UNESCO Secretary General Federico Mayor. Finally, on 17 November 1999, the 30th General Assembly of UNESCO unanimously resolved that "21st February be proclaimed International Mother Language Day throughout the world to commemorate the martyrs who sacrificed their lives on this very day in 1952."

Awards

Ekushey Heritage Award

The annual Ekushey Heritage Award, introduced in 2014 by the Bangladesh Heritage and Ethnic Society of Alberta (BHESA), recognizes outstanding achievement in fields such as education, social work, and community service. The award is announced on International Mother Language Day.

Ekushey Youth Award

The Ekushey Youth Award, introduced in 2015 by Alberta's Mahinur Jahid Memorial Foundation (MJMF) and announced on IMLD, is awarded annually to recipients who inspire youth in the fields of education, sports, youth activities, literature, and community service. The award is open to Alberta residents.

International Mother Language Day, first proclaimed by UNESCO and later adopted by the UN General Assembly, underscores the role of languages in promoting inclusion and achieving Sustainable Development Goals. Multilingual education not only promotes inclusive societies but also aids in preserving non-dominant, minority, and indigenous languages. It is a cornerstone for achieving equitable access to education and lifelong learning opportunities for all individuals.

Significance

Commemoration of the Language Movement

The day honors the students and activists who were killed on February 21, 1952, in Dhaka, East Pakistan (now Bangladesh), while protesting for the right to use their mother language, Bangla. It is officially recognized as National Martyr's Day in Bangladesh.

Promotion of Linguistic Diversity

Proclaimed by UNESCO in 1999 and observed annually since 2000, it encourages the preservation of the world's ~7,000 languages, many of which are under threat of extinction.

Importance of Education in Mother Tongue

This day emphasizes that education in the first language is crucial for early learning, cognitive development, and overall educational quality. It is noted that 40% of the global population lacks access to education in their native language.

Cultural Identity and Preservation

Languages are not just communication tools but carriers of cultural traditions, unique modes of thinking, and memory. The day promotes maintaining these languages to prevent the loss of intangible cultural heritage.

Fostering Inclusion and Peace

By promoting multilingualism, the day supports inclusive societies, as linguistic diversity helps in maintaining peace, tolerance, and respect for others.

Celebrations

The Solemn Traditions of Bangladesh

The Barefoot Procession (Probhat Ferri): Starting at the earliest hours of dawn, thousands of people walk barefoot in silent, disciplined processions to the Central Shaheed Minar in Dhaka and its replicas nationwide.

Floral Tributes: Participants carry bouquets to lay at the base of the monument. In 2025, the Central Shaheed Minar saw over a million visitors offering flowers.

Dressing the Part: It is customary to wear traditional black and white clothing as a sign of mourning. Many also wear black badges pinned to their chests.

Street Art (Alpana): Fine arts students and local communities paint intricate, colorful Alpana motifs on the roads leading to the monuments, symbolizing the vibrancy of the language surviving the bloodshed.

The Ekushey Anthem: The mournful song "Amar Bhaier Rokte Rangano Ekushey February" (My Brother's Blood-Stained February 21st) is sung continuously throughout the day.

The Month-Long Celebration: Ekushey Boi Mela

Duration and Scale: Organized by the Bangla Academy, this fair lasts the entire month of February.

Focus: It is a massive gathering of publishers, authors, and readers, designed to celebrate and promote Bengali literature. In 2025, the fair featured a "July Corner" dedicated to the recent mass uprising in the country.

Awards: The Prime Minister typically presents the Ekushey Padak, one of the highest civilian awards in Bangladesh, to individuals for their contributions to Bengali culture and language.

Global Advocacy and UNESCO Events

UNESCO Themes: Each year focuses on a specific policy goal. The 2025 Silver Jubilee theme, "Languages Matter", celebrated 25 years of advocacy for mother-tongue-based education, while the 2026 focus emphasizes the role of young people in shaping future education systems.

Technical Dialogues: At its Paris headquarters, UNESCO hosts two-day events featuring technical sessions, high-level policy dialogues, and cultural performances from around the world.

Linguapax Prize: The Linguapax Institute in Barcelona awards its annual prize on this day to individuals or organizations demonstrating outstanding commitment to linguistic diversity and the revival of endangered languages.

Global Perspectives: The Diaspora & Community Movement

While the heart of the movement remains in Dhaka, International Mother Language Day has transformed into a decentralized global festival. From the legislative halls of Canada to the parks of Sydney, the "spirit of Ekushey" is used to celebrate local linguistic heritage and fight for the survival of minority tongues.

The United Kingdom: A Hub of Linguistic Activism

The UK, particularly in cities with significant South Asian populations, hosts some of the most elaborate tributes outside of Bangladesh.

Londons Altab Ali Park: Located in Whitechapel, this park houses a permanent replica of the Shaheed Minar. Every February 21st, thousands of people gather at midnight, regardless of the weather, to lay wreaths and sing "Amar Bhaier Rokte Rangano". The event is often supported by the Tower Hamlets Council, which organizes "Write Your Own Language" workshops for local youth.

Manchester: The "City of Languages": Manchester is home to over 200 spoken languages. The Manchester City of Literature hosts a multi-week celebration. Highlights include:

The Multilingual Chain Poem: A rolling poem written and recited in dozens of different languages across the city’s libraries.
"Looking for Languages" Trails: Interactive city tours that highlight the history of Yiddish, Romani, and Bengali in the city's fabric.
Mother Tongue Other Tongue: A national poetry competition laureated by Carol Ann Duffy, where students write in their native language and explain its emotional resonance in English.

Canada: Legislative Leadership and Recognition

Canada has a unique historical connection to the day; it was two Bangladeshi-Canadians, Rafiqul Islam and Abdus Salam, who originally wrote to the UN in 1998 proposing the date.

Federal Recognition: In 2022, Canada solidified its commitment through Bill S-214, making International Mother Language Day an officially recognized federal day.

The British Columbia Model: In BC, the First Peoples' Cultural Council uses this day to highlight Indigenous Language Revitalization. They host "Language Nest" demonstrations where elders and toddlers interact in traditional First Nations languages like Haida or Cree to ensure intergenerational transmission.

Australia: Monuments of Inclusion

Australias celebrations focus on the intersection of the Bangladeshi struggle and the preservation of its own First Nations languages.

Sydneys Ashfield Park: This park features a permanent International Mother Language Day Monument. The design is unique—it features a globe with the word "Mother" written in 21 different languages. On the day, the Ekushey Academy Australia organizes a "Language Festival" featuring Aboriginal dance alongside Bengali poetry.

The "Mother Tongue" Film Circuit: Major Australian universities screen documentaries that explore the "stolen generations" and the loss of Aboriginal languages, framing the 1952 movement as a universal struggle for dignity.

The United States: Cultural Preservation

In the U.S., the day is less about public holidays and more about academic and digital preservation.

The Smithsonian Institution: The Mother Tongue Film Festival in Washington, D.C., is the premier U.S. event. It highlights the power of media to save "sleeping" languages.

The International Mother Language Institute (IMLI): Efforts are ongoing to establish more localized research centers that document endangered immigrant languages in diverse hubs like Queens, New York.

2026

Youth voices on multilingual education

The linguistic landscape has undergone profound change in recent years, shaped by increased migration, rapid technological development, and growing recognition of the cognitive, social, and economic benefits of multilingualism. Today, multilingualism is increasingly understood not only as a social reality but as a fundamental human characteristic and a powerful educational approach. Young people play a vital role in this evolution by defending and revitalising languages, creating digital content, and using technology to make linguistic diversity more visible and valued. These efforts reinforce the deep connection between language, identity, learning, well-being, and participation in society, while highlighting the need for education systems that recognise and support learners’ languages.

At the same time, significant challenges remain, as 40% of learners worldwide still lack access to education in a language they understand best, with indigenous, migrant, and minority youth most affected. Addressing this gap requires education policies and practices that embed multilingual education at their core to promote inclusion, equity, and effective learning for all. By advancing concrete actions, sharing promising experiences, and fostering dialogue among young people, educators, and policymakers, global initiatives create spaces to exchange ideas and identify solutions that strengthen linguistic diversity in schools and communities worldwide.

Safeguarding Linguistic Diversity

Languages, with their complex implications for identity, communication, social integration, education and development, are of strategic importance for people and planet. Yet, due to globalization processes, they are increasingly under threat, or disappearing altogether. When languages fade, so does the world's rich tapestry of cultural diversity. Opportunities, traditions, memory, unique modes of thinking and expression — valuable resources for ensuring a better future — are also lost.

Every two weeks a language disappears taking with it an entire cultural and intellectual heritage. UNESCO estimates that there are 8,324 languages, spoken or signed. Out of these, around 7,000 languages are still in use. Only a few hundred languages have genuinely been given a place in education systems and the public domain, and less than a hundred are used in the digital world.

International Mother Language Day is observed every year to promote linguistic and cultural diversity and multilingualism.

Key Focus Areas for 2026

Youth Leadership: Highlighting young people as essential agents for defending mother tongues and creating digital content to make diverse languages more visible.

Access and Equity: Addressing the fact that 40% of learners globally still lack access to education in a language they understand best, particularly affecting indigenous and migrant youth.

Multilingualism as a Right: Promoting education systems that recognize every learner's language to support better learning outcomes, identity, and social participation.

Silver Jubilee Continuation: While 2025 marked the 25th anniversary (Silver Jubilee), 2026 continues to build on this milestone by focusing on sustainability and the International Decade of Indigenous Languages (2022–2032).
